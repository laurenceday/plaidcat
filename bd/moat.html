Defensibility

The moat verdict

Does holding the borrower dataset create a defensible advantage, or do Plaid's contracts, portability, and borrower rights hollow it out? The graveyard of credit protocols answers most of it; the rest is structural.

1 · What the graveyard teaches

Three lessons carry: continuous pull-based data beats self-reported documents; derived-metrics distribution is where the value realizes (Credora's acquirer bought methodology and track record, not raw data); and completeness checks matter more than cryptography, because every blow-up above was a hidden-liability problem, not a proof problem.

2 · The bear case is structural

Say it plainly

Consent-based bank data is re-obtainable and non-exclusive by construction. A competitor with the borrower's consent pulls the same 24 months of history in roughly one API call. Borrowers hold contractual deletion rights over the raw archive. The end user owns the raw data under Plaid's own MSA, and open-banking policy pushes portability further. Raw data is not a moat, and pretending otherwise would misprice the whole effort.

3 · What actually compounds

Longitudinal depth past month 24

Aggregator windows cap at two years. From month 25 the cursor journal holds history no re-link can reproduce; through-a-cycle cash-flow history on the same borrower is unpurchasable. It compounds slowly and matters when tenor and renewal decisions use it.

The consent graph

Standing, monitored, contractually-teethed connections to N borrowers: cure periods, coverage tests, rep letters. An operational asset a competitor must renegotiate borrower by borrower. Real, but shallow: switching friction, not lock-in.

The outcomes dataset

Cash-flow features joined to onchain credit outcomes: repayments, delinquencies, covenant breaches, cure behavior, and market-run dynamics that only Wildcat's seat can observe. Nobody else holds the label side at the institutional-crypto frontier: not the bureaus, not Plaid, not Credora-by-RedStone. This is proprietary training data for credit models, and it is the thick part of the moat.

Cross-borrower structure

The counterparty graph across borrowers (shared customers, shared funding sources, wash-detection) and peer benchmarks are visible only from the platform seat, and they are what clean-room-style analytics can later monetize.

4 · The verdict

Answer to core question 2

Retaining the data creates a defensible advantage only if the layer is built as a label factory. The moat is not the data: raw bank history is a commodity any consented competitor re-pulls, and Plaid's contracts plus borrower deletion rights make the raw archive a license position, not property. It is partly the consent graph, real but shallow. It is mostly the outcomes dataset: continuous cash-flow features joined to onchain credit outcomes at a frontier where no incumbent has labels. The design consequences are concrete: bitemporal raw archive, reproducible metric runs, outcome tables joined from day one, and distribution as signed attestations and feeds, because derived scores are what get consumed. Wildcat's hooks make it the one protocol where those attestations plug directly into market mechanics.

One honest caveat to close on. For crypto-native trading firms, the bank layer alone monitors the wrong pocket: exchange balances and prime-broker exposure live outside bank rails, which is precisely the hole that swallowed Maple and fooled Credora. The moat thesis holds for the data layer as a whole only when exchange and custodian attestations ride alongside the bank feed. Budget for both from the start.
